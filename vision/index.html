Our Vision: Healthcare That Puts People First

Why this moment calls for a healthcare system that is better for our finances and better for the people who depend on it.

Healthcare should be about getting well—not worrying about how to pay for getting well.

Why Medicare for All matters now

America has the medical knowledge, skilled professionals, hospitals, and resources to provide excellent healthcare. Yet millions of people still encounter a second problem when they need treatment: how to pay for it. Our vision is a country where medical care is organized around healing and recovery, not financial anxiety.

A personal reason this matters

A few weeks ago, I spent three days in the hospital after a minor stroke. I am grateful for the care I received. I have what I consider top-of-the-line Medicare Advantage coverage through UnitedHealthcare. Yet even with that coverage, I expect to face thousands of dollars in copayments from this one three-day hospital stay. These are anticipated charges, not bills I have already received. That is my personal experience, and it illustrates a serious weakness in our healthcare financing system: having insurance—even coverage you consider excellent—does not necessarily mean care is affordable.

My story is not unusual

Across the United States, families experience medical emergencies, surgeries, cancer treatment, chronic illnesses, and unexpected hospitalizations. Even insured patients can confront deductibles, copays, coinsurance, bills they did not anticipate, and lost income while recovering. The precise costs vary, but the underlying problem is widespread: illness can become a financial crisis just when people are least able to manage one.

A better financial design

Americans already pay for healthcare through a combination of taxes, insurance premiums, employer contributions, deductibles, and direct medical bills. Medicare for All would change how that money is collected and distributed, using public financing to cover medically necessary care. A carefully designed system could reduce billing complexity, lower some administrative costs, improve bargaining over prices, and eliminate many point-of-care charges. Federal spending and taxes would rise, while premiums and other private payments could fall. Whether the nation and individual households save money depends on the specific legislation and its implementation.

Better for patients, families, and caregivers

Patients should be able to seek timely care without asking whether an appointment, test, prescription, or hospital stay will threaten their household finances. Clinicians should be able to focus more of their attention on treatment and less on insurance paperwork and coverage disputes. Families should be able to concentrate on recovery rather than calculating what an illness will cost. Coverage that follows a person throughout life could also reduce the fear of changing jobs or losing employment-based insurance.

The system must work in practice

Universal coverage alone will not solve every healthcare problem. America would still need enough doctors, nurses, clinics, hospital capacity, fair provider payment, effective oversight, and thoughtful transition planning. A serious Medicare for All proposal must address financing, access, quality, waiting times, and the people whose jobs would change. Those are engineering and policy challenges to solve—not reasons to accept avoidable financial hardship indefinitely.

A promise we can choose to keep

We can build a healthcare system that treats access to care as a shared national responsibility. We can evaluate the choices by results rather than political labels. We can demand a system that is financially responsible and humane. We should not accept a situation in which people who are sick must also fear the bills that follow them home.

There is no them. There is only us.

Willis Butts · Medicare for All
