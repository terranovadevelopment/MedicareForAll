Better Healthcare Could Cost Less Than We Spend Today

Why universal coverage is not necessarily more expensive—and what the research actually shows.

The surprising idea is not that healthcare becomes free. It is that we can pay for healthcare more intelligently.

We are already paying the bill

Many Americans hear “Medicare for All” and immediately ask: How could we possibly afford to cover everyone? The question assumes that universal coverage would be an entirely new expense. But we already pay enormous sums for healthcare through taxes, employer contributions, insurance premiums, deductibles, copayments, and other out-of-pocket bills. The real question is whether a different system could use that existing money more efficiently.

A household may pay part of its healthcare costs through payroll deductions, another part at the pharmacy, and still more through taxes. Businesses also spend money on employee health benefits. Those payments come from different pockets, but they all belong in the nation’s total healthcare bill.

What the research finds

A peer-reviewed 2020 analysis led by Alison Galvani and published in The Lancet modeled a Medicare for All-style system and estimated a 13% reduction in national healthcare spending—more than $450 billion annually in the study’s 2017-dollar framework. The researchers concluded that expanded coverage could be financed while lowering total national spending, largely through administrative efficiencies and lower prices. [1]

The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office examined five illustrative single-payer designs in 2020. For 2030, its scenarios ranged from approximately $700 billion less to $300 billion more in total national healthcare expenditures compared with projected current law. That range matters: savings are possible, but they depend on how the system is designed. [2]

In August 2026, Yale researchers described a newer model projecting roughly $1.04 trillion in annual savings, or nearly 20%, using 2024 spending data. That study was a preprint at the time of Yale’s announcement and had not yet undergone peer review, so its result should be treated as a projection rather than a settled fact. [3]

Where could the savings come from?

Less paperwork and billing complexity. A unified payment structure could reduce the administrative work required to manage many different insurance networks, contracts, claims rules, and billing procedures.

Stronger purchasing power. A large public payer could negotiate or regulate prices for drugs and services, though the impact on providers and investment would need careful consideration.

Earlier, more consistent care. People with reliable access may seek treatment before manageable conditions become emergencies. That can improve health, although it does not guarantee short-term savings in every category.

Fewer financial barriers. Premiums, deductibles, and medical debt are not just accounting entries; they affect whether families obtain care and how secure their finances remain.

The crucial distinction: federal spending versus total spending

Under a single-payer plan, government spending would rise substantially because payments now made by private insurers, employers, and households would shift toward public financing. That does not automatically mean the country would spend more in total. A larger federal budget line can coexist with smaller combined bills for households, businesses, and government. The financing plan—and how taxes replace existing private payments—must be evaluated openly. [2]

What still needs to be answered

No study can guarantee a particular savings figure. Policymakers must decide provider payment rates, drug prices, long-term care benefits, taxes, and how to maintain timely access when more people seek treatment. CBO warned that some designs could create additional pressure on appointment availability even while expanding coverage. [2]

The responsible comparison is not “new government cost” versus “zero cost.” It is the full cost and quality of the current system versus the full cost and quality of a proposed replacement—including who pays, who is covered, and what happens to patients and healthcare workers.

The realization

The United States does not need to discover a new source of money before it can discuss universal healthcare. It needs to ask whether the money already flowing through healthcare can buy broader coverage, simpler administration, and better value. Credible studies show that spending less overall is possible. The challenge is designing and implementing a system that actually delivers those savings.
