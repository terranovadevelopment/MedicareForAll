Article 29: Insured but Still Unable to Afford Care: The Walmart Worker’s Dilemma

Why having health insurance does not always mean affordable access to care.

Download illustrated Word document (.docx)

Walmart is a useful case study of the gap between insurance enrollment and affordable access to treatment.

The insurance card is not the finish line

Walmart offers health insurance and other healthcare benefits to eligible employees. But the more important question is whether a worker can afford to use that coverage when illness strikes. Insurance may protect against some large expenses while still requiring premiums, deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance. For an hourly employee, those costs compete with rent, food, transportation, and utilities.

What the paycheck reveals

The accompanying Walmart worker analysis describes a hypothetical full-time associate earning $18 an hour, or $37,440 annually before taxes. It cites a 2026 Premier PPO employee premium of $38.30 every two weeks for individual coverage—about $996 a year. The cited premium for family coverage is $227 every two weeks, or $5,902 annually. That family premium alone is about 15.8% of this hypothetical worker’s gross pay. These are plan-specific figures, not a claim about every Walmart employee.

The cost of actually using the insurance

Premiums buy coverage; they do not necessarily pay every medical bill. Depending on the plan and service, an insured worker may still owe a deductible, copayment, or coinsurance for tests, imaging, hospital care, or prescriptions. Consider a hypothetical family that pays $5,902 in premiums and another $3,000 for covered medical care. That is $8,902 in one year—about 23.8% of the worker’s $37,440 gross income. The additional $3,000 is an illustration, not a documented Walmart average.

Why some people consider paying cash

When patients face deductibles, limited networks, or confusing bills, they may compare a provider’s self-pay price with what they expect to owe using insurance. In some circumstances, a transparent cash price can appear simpler or lower. But self-pay is not automatically cheaper, and cash payments may not count toward a plan’s deductible or out-of-pocket maximum. Patients should compare the actual terms before choosing. The supplied Walmart analysis does not establish how many Walmart workers use self-pay instead of insurance.

Delayed care can become more expensive care

An employee who fears a bill may postpone an appointment, diagnostic test, or prescription. A manageable problem can become harder to treat, sometimes resulting in urgent or emergency care and missed work. That pattern is a recognized concern in healthcare affordability debates, although it cannot be assumed to describe every Walmart associate. The human cost matters as much as the financial one: people should be encouraged to seek appropriate care early, not punished for doing so.

The coverage gap for part-time workers

According to the supplied analysis, most part-time hourly store associates generally must average at least 30 hours weekly during the applicable measurement period to qualify for Walmart medical benefits, with exceptions for certain positions. A worker with fewer hours may have to rely on another coverage source. The connection between work schedules and insurance eligibility adds uncertainty for workers whose hours change.

A fair look at Walmart’s benefits

Walmart does offer meaningful assistance, including virtual care, mental health services, and specialized programs for qualifying conditions. Some covered services may have no deductible or copayment. Those benefits deserve recognition. The criticism here is not that Walmart provides no insurance. It is that even a company offering substantial benefits operates within a larger system in which being insured does not always mean that care is affordable.

What universal healthcare could change

A comprehensive Medicare for All proposal would separate eligibility for covered healthcare from the number of hours someone works or the employer they work for. Proposals commonly seek to eliminate separate premiums, deductibles, and copayments for covered services. Public financing would replace much of the current insurance financing, so the actual household and employer effects would depend on the legislation and tax design.

The question we should be asking

The national goal should not merely be to count how many workers possess an insurance card. We should ask how many can afford an appointment, fill a prescription, follow a treatment plan, and recover without financial hardship. That is the difference between nominal insurance coverage and genuine healthcare security.

Source note: Based on “Walmart workers and their healthcare experience,” supplied by the author. Its wage and premium figures are presented as described in that document; hypothetical examples are explicitly labeled. The document does not provide a survey measuring employee self-pay behavior.
