Medicare for All: What It Could Mean for Texas

A historic election, a healthcare crisis, and an opportunity to put Texans first

By Willis Butts | Terra Nova Development, Inc. | November 10, 2026

Download Word article (.docx)

By Willis Butts | Terra Nova Development, Inc. | November 10, 2026

A Turning Point for Texas

Texas has just elected Gina Hinojosa as its next governor, ending more than three decades without a Democratic governor. She is expected to take office in January 2027. Her victory creates an opportunity for a new discussion about affordability, public services, and healthcare. But a change in governor does not, by itself, enact Medicare for All. That would require federal legislation, and I have not found sufficient evidence to describe Hinojosa as an explicit supporter of the specific federal single-payer bill. The important question is what a governor and a state can do to move Texans toward affordable, reliable care while the national debate continues.

The Texas Problem Is Too Large to Ignore

In 2024, 19.2 percent of Texans under age 65 lacked health insurance, the highest rate in the country and nearly twice the national rate of 9.8 percent, according to KFF. Texas also remained among the states that had not adopted the Affordable Care Act’s Medicaid expansion as of March 2026. Those numbers represent people: a parent postponing a checkup, a worker ignoring symptoms, or a family facing a bill it cannot pay. Insurance coverage is not the same as access, either. Even insured Texans can struggle with deductibles, narrow networks, and medical debt.

What Medicare for All Would Change

Under a comprehensive national Medicare for All model, residents would have coverage independent of their jobs, income, or age. Covered care would be financed through public funding rather than a patchwork of private premiums, deductibles, and copayments. The details—including benefits, provider payment, transition rules, and financing—would depend on the legislation Congress actually passes. A well-designed system could remove much of the financial uncertainty that now follows a diagnosis. Nobody chooses to develop cancer, suffer a stroke, or have a child born with complex medical needs. Illness should not be a financial punishment.

For Working Families and Retirees

Texas families could gain freedom from the fear of losing health coverage when a job changes or disappears. Workers could take a better job, start a business, or retire without making health insurance the deciding factor. People who already receive Medicare would need clear assurances that their coverage, provider access, and benefits would be protected or improved during any transition. The goal should be simple: care based on medical need, not employment status or ability to pay at the clinic door.

For Texas Businesses and the Economy

Employers currently devote money and staff time to selecting health plans, managing enrollment, and absorbing unpredictable premium increases. A national single-payer system could simplify that burden, particularly for small businesses competing with larger employers. But those savings are not free money: public financing would replace much of today’s private spending. The net effect on any household or business would depend on tax design, wages, current benefit costs, and the final law. Texans deserve a transparent accounting that compares total costs—not just taxes in isolation.

For Rural Texas and Healthcare Professionals

In rural communities, the existence of an insurance card does not guarantee a nearby doctor or hospital. Any Medicare for All plan must pay serious attention to rural hospital financing, primary care staffing, mental health services, emergency transport, and specialist shortages. Simplified billing could free healthcare workers from some administrative tasks, but payment rates must be designed so essential facilities remain viable. Coverage reform and healthcare capacity have to advance together.

Medical Debt Must Be Part of the Solution

Universal coverage would help prevent future medical debt, but it would not automatically erase bills that families already owe. I favor a separate national medical-debt relief program that removes the payment obligation from patients while compensating legitimate creditors under carefully negotiated terms. One possible approach would refinance verified medical debts at zero interest, with the government making scheduled payments. Any such plan would require safeguards against inflated claims, duplicate collections, and unfair payouts. Healthcare reform should address yesterday’s financial damage as well as tomorrow’s medical needs.

What a New Texas Governor Can Actually Do

A Texas governor cannot establish federal Medicare for All alone. However, the incoming administration could champion Medicaid expansion, work with the Legislature to improve enrollment and continuity of coverage, support rural health services, strengthen medical-billing protections, and advocate nationally for universal coverage. These steps would be meaningful even if Congress does not enact single-payer reform immediately. They would also give Texas practical experience reducing gaps in care. The exact agenda will depend on the governor-elect’s proposals, legislative support, and budget choices.

A Systems-Engineering Approach

As a retired systems engineer, I see healthcare as a system whose purpose should be to deliver timely, effective care to everyone who needs it. We should measure the entire system: total household costs, employer costs, government spending, administrative overhead, provider capacity, health outcomes, and medical debt. A reform that lowers one line item but shifts greater costs elsewhere is not necessarily an improvement. The goal is to redesign the system around the patient, reduce needless complexity, and make its performance visible to the public.

The Opportunity Before Us

Texas can become a leader in asking a fundamental question: Why should getting sick threaten a family's financial future? The election of a new governor creates an opening for that conversation, but lasting change will require evidence, legislation, and cooperation across political differences. Texans disagree on many issues, yet illness can reach every family. We can debate financing and implementation while agreeing that people deserve care and dignity. There is no them. There is only us.
