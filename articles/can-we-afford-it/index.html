Medicare for All: Can America Afford It—or Can We Afford Not to Change?

The economics of universal healthcare, the hidden costs of our current system, and the opportunity to provide broader coverage while controlling national spending.

The Question We Keep Asking Backward

Whenever Medicare for All is proposed, one objection appears almost immediately: “How are we going to pay for it?”

It is an important question. Any proposal to reorganize something as large as America’s healthcare system deserves serious financial scrutiny.

But there is another question that deserves equal attention: How are we going to continue paying for the healthcare system we already have?

Americans are not waiting for Medicare for All to begin spending enormous sums on healthcare. We are already paying through federal taxes, state taxes, employer insurance contributions, employee premiums, deductibles, copayments, prescription costs, and medical bills.

The money is already flowing. The issue is how effectively we use it.

Medicare for All proposes changing the way healthcare is financed, replacing much of today’s fragmented insurance system with a unified public financing arrangement.

The central economic argument is straightforward: if we can eliminate unnecessary administrative costs, negotiate more effectively, and simplify payment systems, we may be able to provide broader coverage without increasing the nation’s total healthcare bill.

Whether a particular Medicare for All proposal accomplishes that depends on its design. But the potential is substantial.

We Are Already Spending Trillions

According to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, the United States spent approximately $5.3 trillion on healthcare in 2024.

That represented about $15,474 per person, approximately 18% of the entire U.S. economy, and spending through government programs, private insurance, employers, households, and other sources.

These are not hypothetical future costs. They represent what America was already spending.

And the financial pressure is expected to grow. In projections published in 2026, CMS estimated that national healthcare expenditures could reach approximately $9 trillion annually by 2034, consuming 20.6% of the economy.

That is the trajectory against which any alternative system should be evaluated.

The relevant comparison is not Medicare for All versus spending nothing. It is Medicare for All versus continuing to finance the system we already have.

The Difference Between Government Spending and National Spending

One of the most common misunderstandings in the Medicare for All debate involves the difference between government spending and total healthcare spending.

Under our current system, a large share of healthcare is financed privately. Employers pay insurance companies. Workers contribute premiums. Patients pay deductibles and copayments.

Under Medicare for All, much of that private financing would be replaced by public financing. Consequently, federal spending would increase dramatically.

That fact alone does not establish that healthcare would become more expensive for the country.

Consider a simplified example. Suppose a family currently pays $8,000 annually in direct insurance premiums and medical expenses, while its employer contributes another $18,000 toward insurance. The combined cost is $26,000.

Now imagine a different financing arrangement in which those premiums are eliminated and replaced with taxes totaling $21,000 for the same household and employer combined. In that hypothetical example, government collections increase, but the combined financial burden decreases by $5,000.

The actual outcome would vary by household, employer, income, and tax structure. Some would pay more, others less.

The example illustrates a fundamental accounting principle: Moving an expense from private payments to public financing does not automatically create a new expense for the economy.

We must evaluate the combined cost, not simply the federal budget.

The Cost of Maintaining Thousands of Insurance Arrangements

America’s healthcare financing system is extraordinarily complicated.

Hospitals and physicians must deal with numerous insurance companies, each with its own billing requirements, reimbursement rules, provider networks, prior-authorization procedures, and appeals processes.

Entire administrative departments exist to navigate these arrangements. Insurance companies also maintain extensive administrative operations.

A unified financing system could simplify many of these processes. Hospitals could spend less time determining which insurance company owes what. Physicians could devote fewer resources to claim disputes. Patients could spend less time navigating coverage restrictions.

Not every administrative expense would disappear. Medicare for All would still require systems to process claims, prevent fraud, maintain records, and manage payments.

But eliminating duplicative administrative work is one of the principal mechanisms through which a single-payer system could reduce costs. Those savings would be available to help finance broader coverage.

What Businesses Are Paying Today

Healthcare financing is not merely a household issue. It is also a major business expense.

According to KFF’s 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey, the average annual premium for employer-sponsored family coverage reached $26,993. Workers contributed an average of $6,850 directly, with employers paying the remaining $20,143.

For businesses, healthcare benefits represent a substantial labor expense. For employees, insurance can influence decisions about where to work, whether to change jobs, and whether to start a business.

A Medicare for All system could separate health coverage from employment. Workers would no longer need to remain in a particular job simply to preserve their health insurance.

Small businesses could compete for employees without having to organize their own comprehensive insurance arrangements. Entrepreneurs could establish businesses without first solving the problem of family health coverage.

These potential benefits are economically significant, although the actual savings for businesses would depend on the taxes used to finance the new system.

The important point is that employers are already helping finance healthcare. Any new financing proposal must be compared against those existing obligations.

Better Coverage Does Not Necessarily Mean Higher Total Costs

Medicare for All proposals generally seek to provide comprehensive coverage without the premiums, deductibles, and copayments common in private insurance.

Depending on the legislation, covered services may include hospital care, physician visits, preventive services, prescription medications, mental healthcare, dental care, vision care, and other medically necessary services.

That would represent a significant expansion of financial protection for many Americans. But broader coverage also has a cost.

People who previously delayed care because they could not afford it would be more likely to obtain treatment. Some of that additional care would increase spending.

The economic question is whether savings elsewhere could offset those increases. Potential sources of savings include lower administrative expenses, negotiated pharmaceutical prices, standardized provider payments, and reduced billing complexity.

The net result depends on the balance between those savings and the cost of expanded services. Broader coverage and lower national spending are compatible goals, but they are not automatic outcomes.

What the Economic Studies Actually Tell Us

Researchers have studied several versions of single-payer healthcare financing.

Some models project that national healthcare expenditures would decline relative to the current system. Others find that spending could increase, particularly if provider payments remain high, demand rises substantially, or the proposed savings do not materialize.

The Congressional Budget Office has examined how single-payer systems could change healthcare utilization, administrative spending, provider payments, and government financing.

The broad lesson is that the details matter. A system that reduces administrative expenses and negotiates prices effectively may produce substantial savings. A system that expands benefits without sufficient cost controls may not.

Therefore, the strongest economic case for Medicare for All is not that every conceivable version would save money.

It is that America already spends enough on healthcare to make a comprehensive restructuring of how that money is used worthy of serious consideration.

The Hidden Costs of Delayed Care

Healthcare spending is not the only economic measure that matters.

When people delay treatment because they cannot afford it, the consequences can extend beyond medical bills.

A manageable health problem may become more serious. An employee may miss work. A family may lose income. An untreated condition may eventually require more expensive intervention.

Earlier access to preventive and primary care can improve outcomes and sometimes reduce subsequent costs.

However, preventive care does not always save money in purely financial terms. Some preventive services increase total expenditures while producing important health benefits.

That distinction matters. A healthcare system should be evaluated by both its cost and the health outcomes it delivers.

The objective is not simply to spend less. It is to obtain greater value from the money we spend.

The Challenge of Financing the Transition

Moving to Medicare for All would be a major undertaking.

A workable proposal would need to address several difficult questions.

How would federal taxes replace premiums and other private payments? How would hospitals, physicians, and dentists be reimbursed? How would the system ensure enough medical professionals and facilities remain available? How would employees currently working in private insurance administration be supported through the transition?

There would also be questions about state Medicaid funding, existing Medicare programs, prescription drug pricing, and the financial stability of rural hospitals.

These are legitimate challenges, not reasons to ignore the underlying economics. They are engineering, administrative, and public-finance problems that require careful design.

The financial test should be transparent: what would households, businesses, and governments pay under the new arrangement, and what would they otherwise pay under the current system?

The Cost of Doing Nothing

The current healthcare system also carries substantial financial risks.

Healthcare spending continues to consume a large share of national income. Employer premiums remain expensive. Many households face significant medical expenses even when they have insurance.

Continuing the existing arrangement is not a cost-free decision.

It means accepting the future costs, administrative burdens, and coverage limitations associated with that arrangement unless other reforms are adopted.

Medicare for All is one proposed response. Other approaches include expanded public insurance options, stronger price regulation, insurance-market reforms, and targeted improvements to existing programs.

Each alternative deserves evaluation against the same measures: national spending, coverage, access, quality, financing, and implementation.

Can We Afford Not to Change?

The phrase “We can’t afford Medicare for All” often treats the cost of a new system as though America is starting from zero.

We are not. We are starting from a healthcare economy already spending trillions of dollars every year.

That reality changes the conversation.

The challenge is to determine whether a more coordinated financing system can use existing healthcare resources more efficiently, protect families from medical bills, reduce administrative burdens, and provide reliable access to care.

A carefully designed Medicare for All program could potentially achieve those goals while reducing national expenditures relative to projected spending under the current system.

But the savings must be demonstrated through credible assumptions, transparent financing, and realistic implementation plans.

The most important question is not simply: “Can we afford Medicare for All?”

It is: “What will it cost to provide healthcare under each available system—and what will Americans receive for that money?”

At this point in our history, the financial consequences of continuing the current system deserve every bit as much scrutiny as the cost of replacing it.
