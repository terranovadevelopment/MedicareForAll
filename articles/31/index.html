Article 31: Choose Your Doctor, Not Your Insurance Network

Freedom to choose a doctor without insurance-network barriers

Download illustrated Word document (.docx)

Why universal healthcare should put the patient–doctor relationship first

The illustration presents the goal of comprehensive doctor coverage without patient cost-sharing. Exact benefits would depend on the law enacted.

A simple promise

When someone needs medical care, the first question should be, “Which doctor is best qualified to help me?” It should not be, “Which doctor is in my insurance network, and how much will the visit cost?” A comprehensive Medicare for All system could change that conversation. Its promise is not merely an insurance card for everyone. It is dependable access to medical professionals without financial obstacles standing between patients and necessary treatment.

The problem with insurance networks

Today, many Americans must check whether a physician, specialist, clinic, or hospital participates in a particular insurance plan. A doctor who treated a patient for years may become out of network after an employer changes plans. A patient may face a higher bill for seeing an out-of-network specialist, or discover that a referral or prior authorization is required. Networks can help insurers manage prices and coordinate care, but they can also complicate access and interrupt relationships that matter.

A patient should not lose a doctor when a job changes

Employment-based insurance ties healthcare arrangements to decisions made by employers and insurers. Workers can lose access to familiar clinicians when they change jobs, retire, reduce hours, or start businesses. That is especially difficult for people managing diabetes, heart disease, cancer, mental illness, or other conditions requiring continuous care. Coverage that follows the person rather than the job would remove one major source of disruption.

Primary care, specialists, and prevention

Comprehensive coverage should make routine primary care and family medicine accessible, alongside medically necessary specialist consultations. Preventive screenings, vaccinations, chronic disease management, pediatric care, and geriatric care belong in a coherent system. Earlier treatment can prevent some complications and reduce suffering, though expanding access also requires sufficient clinicians, appointments, and facilities.

What “choose your doctor” really means

A well-designed national program could eliminate private-insurance network restrictions for covered services, allowing patients to seek care from participating clinicians nationwide. That is different from guaranteeing an appointment with every doctor. Physicians must agree to participate, accept patients, and have available capacity. Geographic shortages, specialist wait times, and hospital staffing would still need attention. The policy objective should be broad provider choice—not an unrealistic promise that every clinician will always be available.

No bill at the point of care

Under comprehensive single-payer proposals, separate premiums, deductibles, and copayments for covered medical services would generally be eliminated. Patients could make decisions about necessary treatment without first calculating a deductible or fearing an unexpected coinsurance charge. That protection would be especially valuable to families living paycheck to paycheck and retirees on fixed incomes. But “no patient bill” does not mean care costs nothing: physicians, nurses, facilities, and medicines must still be paid through the program’s public financing.

Less paperwork for patients and doctors

A more unified payment system could reduce the time medical offices spend navigating multiple insurers, checking benefits, submitting differing claims, and appealing denials. The potential savings are meaningful, but they depend on actual program rules, payment processes, and implementation. Simpler administration should be a deliberate design goal, not merely an assumption.

Continuity is part of quality

A physician who knows a patient’s history may recognize changes earlier, avoid unnecessary repetition, and coordinate treatment more effectively. Stable relationships also build trust. When insurance rules force patients to change doctors, the costs can include duplicated tests, delayed referrals, and lost continuity—not just higher bills. Universal healthcare should protect the patient–doctor relationship as a foundation of good medicine.

The system must work for medical professionals, too

Coverage expansion requires enough doctors, nurses, therapists, and other professionals to serve patients. Policymakers must consider fair reimbursement, workforce training, rural access, hospital budgets, and administrative workload. A plan that eliminates insurance barriers but leaves communities without available clinicians would fall short of its promise.

A better definition of healthcare freedom

Healthcare freedom should mean being able to seek appropriate medical care, maintain a trusted physician, and follow recommended treatment without financial fear. It should not depend on whether an employer selected the right insurance network. A comprehensive Medicare for All system offers a way to pursue that freedom, provided its financing and delivery systems are designed carefully.

Conclusion: care before coverage rules

The doctor–patient relationship should be about diagnosis, treatment, prevention, and recovery. Insurance paperwork and financial anxiety should not dominate the encounter. Our goal should be a universal healthcare system in which every person can obtain medically necessary care from participating providers, without deductibles or copayments for covered services. Choose your doctor—not your insurance network. And let the medical conversation be about getting well.
